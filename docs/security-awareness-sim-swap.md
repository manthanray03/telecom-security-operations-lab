# Security awareness: SIM swap fraud

**For Customer Care and store staff | Prairie Lynx Telecom (fictional) | 2-minute read**

## What happened

Three customers lost control of their accounts in one week. Someone convinced us to move their phone number to a new SIM, then used the codes we text to that number to take over their online account and request a port-out or a new phone. We caught all three, but one device had already shipped.

## How it works

1. The fraudster collects details about the customer: name, address, date of birth, sometimes the last bill amount.
2. They call us or visit a store pretending to be the customer and ask for a new SIM.
3. Once the number is on their SIM, every text code goes to them. They reset the password, change the email, then port the number away or order a phone.

## Red flags

- The caller is in a hurry, upset, or says they are travelling and their phone was stolen.
- They know the security answers but get small details wrong, or read them slowly.
- The request comes late in the day or just before closing.
- They want the email or shipping address changed in the same call.
- In store: ID that looks new, damaged or doesn't quite match, or a different signature.

## What to do

- **By phone:** don't do a SIM swap on security questions alone. Send a one-time code to the current SIM, or ask the customer to come to a store with photo ID.
- **In store:** check the photo, name and date of birth against the account. If anything doesn't match, stop and call your manager.
- **Never** approve your own SIM swap. If your profile lets you, tell your manager so it can be fixed.
- **Report it:** if something feels wrong after you've done a swap, call Corporate Security right away (internal line 7777, lab example). Reporting quickly is how we stopped the port-out this week.

## Why it matters

A customer's phone number unlocks their bank, email and social media. One fraudulent swap can cost them far more than a phone bill, and it costs us their trust.
